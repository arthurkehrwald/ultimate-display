\subsection{Multiscopic displays}
One of the earliest forays into 3D display technology was the stereoscope. Invented in 1832 by Charles Wheatstone\cite{wheatstone1838contributions} and later improved by David Brewster, \cite{brewster1856stereoscope} this small, handheld device was the first to provide binocular disparity by optically combining through mirrors or lenses two photographs or drawings with horizontally offset perspectives (a stereo pair) into one stereo image suitable for binocular viewing. To this day, VR headsets operate by the same optical principle. More generally, manufacturers of consumer 3D displays focus on providing the binocular depth cue over motion parallax or accommodation, because displaying two images instead of one is often possible with minimal changes to display hardware originally developed for monoscopic images. Much of the manufacturing process is therefore shared with 2D-displays produced in much larger volumes, bringing down the cost per unit compared to displays designed from the ground up for 3D. Consumer stereo displays are also often capable of operating in monoscopic mode as well making them more flexible and therefore more suitable for the mass market.

Concerning depth cues, stereoscopic displays extend the pictorial cues of monoscopic displays by providing binocular disparity and (as a result) vergence, but not accommodation and motion parallax. Similarly, considering a stereoscopic display as an approximation of the plenoptic function, it fails to provide the correct radiance output for all but two sets of $x, y, z$ parameters corresponding to the focal points of the two stereo images. Motion parallax may be added in two ways: Head tracking systems have been used to match the image perspectives to a tracked head position on the fly, but this only works when displaying real-time graphics to a single user. Alternatively, the concept of stereoscopy can be generalized to create multiscopic displays with potentially dozens of views. Unlike stereoscopic displays, that typically use special eyeglasses to ensure each image reaches the intended eye, multiscopic displays emit each view in a controlled range of directions, dividing the space in front of the display into viewing zones. If each displayed perspective matches the location of its corresponding viewing zone, a discrete approximation of motion parallax is achievable. If the viewing zones are additionally more narrow than the viewer's interocular distance at the intended viewing distance, binocular disparity is maintained. Adding more views improves the motion parallax effect, but also typically lowers the visual fidelity of each individual view and increases cost. Due to this tradeoff and the tendencies of users to keep their heads steady and at the same height, many multiscopic displays are intentionally limited to horizontal parallax only.
