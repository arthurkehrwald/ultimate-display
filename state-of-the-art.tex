\section{State of the art}
Along the way towards that ideal, many displays have been developed (and some commercialized), that fulfill some of the requirements outlined in the previous section. For this discussion, they will be categorized into multiscopic, volumetric and holographic designs. This taxonomy mostly follows that proposed by Jason Geng in 2011 \cite{Geng_2013}. The exception is that eyeglasses based stereoscopic displays are not considered as a category of their own and instead discussed alongside multiview displays. Multiview displays approximate the target light field using discrete 2D views. Volumetric displays are a straightforward extension of 2D displays into the third dimension. Where 2D displays have a two-dimensional grid of pixels, volumetric displays have a three-dimensional grid of voxels. Holographic displays directly reconstruct a wavefront using interference patterns. Since there is no sampling via voxels or discrete views, this approach is theoretically ideal, but in practice, many compromises are necessary due to the immense data rate required to display holographic diffraction gratings \cite{Blanche_2021}.

\subsection{Multiview displays}
One of the earliest forays into 3D display technology was the stereoscope. Invented in 1832 by Charles Wheatstone \cite{wheatstone1838contributions} and later improved by David Brewster \cite{brewster1856stereoscope}, this small, handheld device was the first to provide binocular disparity by optically combining through mirrors or lenses two photographs or drawings with horizontally offset perspectives (a stereo pair) into one stereo image suitable for binocular viewing. To this day, VR headsets operate by the same optical principle. More generally, manufacturers of consumer 3D displays focus on providing the binocular depth cue over motion parallax or accommodation, because displaying two images instead of one is often possible with minimal changes to display hardware originally developed for monoscopic images. Much of the manufacturing process is therefore shared with 2D-displays produced in much larger volumes, bringing down the cost per unit compared to displays designed from the ground up for 3D. Consumer stereo displays are also often capable of operating in monoscopic mode as well making them more flexible and therefore more suitable for the mass market.

There are several key technical decisions that define a multiscopic display. As discussed in the previous section, the number of discrete views is the most impactful from the user's point of view, because it determines the presence and quality of binocular disparity and motion parallax. Another important question is how the images are combined to appear in the same place. One option is to optically combine the outputs of multiple 2D displays. For example, multiple projectors may be aimed at the same surface so that their images coincide or the image of one screen may be superimposed onto another using a beam splitter \cite{Balasubramonian_Gunasekaran_Nithiyanandam_Rajappan_1983}. The more common alternative is to show multiple views on a single display through some multiplexing method. The displays pixels (spatial multiplexing), refresh rate (temporal multiplexing), or color space (color multiplexing) are divided into separate image channels. This degrades image quality with each additional view, but makes for a much less bulky and expensive setup.

Once the views are combined on one surface, the display needs some way to prevent crosstalk by either steering the views in different directions or filtering them using special 3D glasses. Many multiscopic displays form their viewing zones by placing a refractive optical element such as a lenticular lens array in front of the pixel matrix. A more primitive variant is the parallax barrier. It simply occludes all but one view depending on the viewing angle. Occlusion is also used in LC-shutter glasses in combination with temporally multiplexed stereoscopic displays to block the left eye while the right image is displayed and vice versa. Polarized 3D glasses serve a similar purpose in combination with phase retarders affixed to the display. In temporally multiplexed stereo displays, an active retarder in combination with a circular polarizer modulates the phase of all light emitted by the display in sync with the frame transitions such that each image is polarized to pass through the correct filter of the 3D glasses worn by the viewer. In spatially multiplexed displays, a passive retarder with a fixed (usually stripe) pattern precisely aligned to the pixel raster of the display changes the polarization of certain pixels (typically alternating rows) to match the filters in the 3D glasses. Concerning depth cues, stereoscopic displays extend the pictorial cues of monoscopic displays by providing binocular disparity. In general, vergence follows from binocular disparity, because the left and right eyes turn towards corresponding image points. In stereo images, corresponding points are shifted horizontally in proportion to their distance from the image plane. If the human visual system recognizes the correspondence, the eyes will turn such that their optical axes intersect the image plane at these shifted points and intersect each other at the vergence point, which is thereby moved behind or in front of the image plane, where the image point in question is meant to appear. As a result, the angle between the sight lines is correlated to the displayed image depth, which is the definition of the vergence depth cue. Unlike vergence and binocular disparity, stereoscopic displays do not exhibit motion parallax. The relative location of objects at different depths remains the same and the perspectives of the two views do not change from different angles of observation. Similarly, considering a stereoscopic display as an approximation of the plenoptic function, it fails to provide the correct radiance output for all but two sets of $x, y, z$ parameters corresponding to the focal points of the two stereo images.

Motion parallax may be added in two ways: Head tracking systems have been used to match the image perspectives to a tracked head position on the fly \cite{Woodgate_Ezra_Harrold_Holliman_Jones_Moseley_1997, Hentschke_2010}, but this only works when displaying real-time graphics to a single user. Alternatively, the concept of stereoscopy can be generalized to create multiscopic displays with potentially dozens of views. Unlike stereoscopic displays, that typically use special eyeglasses to ensure each image reaches the intended eye, multiscopic displays emit each view in a controlled range of directions, dividing the space in front of the display into viewing zones. If each displayed perspective matches the location of its corresponding viewing zone, a discrete approximation of motion parallax is achievable. If the viewing zones are additionally more narrow than the viewer's interocular distance at the intended viewing distance, binocular disparity is maintained. Adding more views improves the motion parallax effect, but also typically lowers the visual fidelity of each individual view and increases cost. Due to this tradeoff and the tendencies of users to keep their heads steady and at the same height, many multiscopic displays are intentionally limited to horizontal parallax only.

Even with the addition of motion parallax, multiscopic displays still lack the accommodation cue. The image appears sharpest at the depth of the display rather than the depth of the image contents, causing the eye lenses to focus on the display surface instead of the image. This is especially significant because stereoscopic images do provide the vergence cue. The vergence point and the focal plane are therefore at different depths, which does not occur in 2D displays or natural viewing. The resulting so called vergence-accommodation conflict has been shown to cause eye strain when viewing stereoscopic imagery for extended periods of time \cite{hoffman2008vergence}. This effect may be mitigated by limiting the maximum disparity and thereby the image depth \cite{ijsselsteijn2000effects}, but the only real solution within multiscopic displays found so far is to provide the accommodation cue by fulfilling the so called \textit{super-multiview  display condition} \cite{kajiki1997hologramlike}. It demands that light from at least two very similar views enter the pupil at the same time. The implication is that there must be so many views that the viewing zones are smaller than the pupil diameter of the human eye (5mm on average). The first prototype to achieve this produced 45 views across a width of 18 cm. Later displays increased the number of views into the hundreds, producing imperceptibly smooth view transitions in motion \cite{Takaki_Nago_2010} It is theorized but not experimentally verified that super multiview displays eliminate the vergence accommodation conflict.

In summary, stereoscopic and multiscopic displays are a practical and commercially viable improvement over conventional 2D displays, although all but a select few fall short of fulfilling the formal requirements for the ultimate display. They all offer binocular disparity and vergence cues. Some additionally provide motion parallax on one or two axes and a few lab prototypes support the accommodation cue as well, although such displays have proven too complex and expensive for commercialization so far \cite{Wen_Qiao_Fengbin_Zhou_Linsen_Chen_2020}.

\subsection{Volumetric displays}

While multiview displays attempt to approximate the plenoptic function using a number of discrete 2D views, volumetric displays forgo two-dimensional images altogether. Instead, the image is directly formed in 3D space by optical elements arranged in a volume rather than on a planar surface. This has the advantage that no further effort is required to provide all physiological depth cues, including accommodation and motion parallax, which are difficult to convey using multiview displays. A fundamental disadvantage of volumetric displays is that the display volume is limited by the size of the display in all three dimensions. Multiview displays are able to act as a ``window to the world'' in that they are capable of displaying scenes that extend arbitrarily far into the space behind the display. In contrast, volumetric displays are fundamentally unable to display anything outside the bounds of their view volume. Even without considering technical difficulties in conversion from 2D formats to volumetric images, the scenes depicted in many applications of 2D display technology simply don't fit into any reasonably sized viewing volume without scaling them down. Despite this limitation, it is worth examining volumetric displays for their inherent ability to provide all physiological depth cues.

The requirement for a volumetric display is to have addressable voxels distributed across the view volume. This may either be achieved by actually placing the voxels in a 3D pattern to begin with (static volume displays), or by sweeping a 2D surface through the volume at high speed to form ``virtual'' voxels addressable only for a short time (swept volume displays). Table \ref{tab:volumetric} categorizes volumetric designs according to this distinction and additionally based on whether the view volume contains active or passive optical elements. Active optical elements include optical fiber \cite{MacFarlane_1994}, LEDs \cite{Schipper_Coddington_1963}, lasers \cite{Momiuchi_Kimura_2009}, CRTs \cite{Ketchpel_1964} as well as LCD \cite{Sullivan_2004} or OLED \cite{Koo_Kim_2009} panels. Projection screens\cite{geng2013volumetric}, mirrors \cite{Sher_1978}, glass substrates \cite{Lewis_Verber_McGhee_1971}, and gas media \cite{Korevaar_Spivey_1989} are examples of passive optical elements. Some of the earliest volumetric displays were swept volume displays \cite{Max_1961, Schipper_Coddington_1963, Ketchpel_1964} adapting known 2D display technology to three dimensions by spinning it in a circle. This approach is straightforward but has numerous disadvantages \cite{Geng_2013}:

\begin{itemize}
    \item Moving parts are expensive and prone to defects.
    \item addressing the voxels is very time sensitive.
    \item Voxels must be very bright because they are only active for a short time.
    \item Non-uniform voxel density.
    \item Moving optical elements tend to cause jitter and blur in the image.
    \item The display shape and size is dictated by the sweeping mechanism.
\end{itemize}

The alternative are static volume displays. Passive variants include various designs based on the upconversion principle. (Refer to bottom right cell in table \ref{tab:volumetric} for examples.) Two lasers with different, invisible wavelengths are intersected at each voxel location. The passive view volume medium has the special property that it responds to the combination of those wavelengths by emitting light at a different, visible wavelength. This effect, known as two-step upconversion, was first observed in the 1920s in mercury vapor \cite{fuchtbauer1920excitation} and later discovered in materials more suitable as a volumetric display medium. Active static volume displays (bottom left cell in table \ref{tab:volumetric}) mostly extend 2D pixel grids into a volume by stacking them. Static volume displays do not have any of the problems caused by moving parts described above. But instead, they often suffer from view-dependent self-occlusion or other forms of interference between voxels, high bandwidth requirements, and manufacturing challenges. Additionally a static view volume alone does not mean all voxels can be active in parallel. Particularly in the case of static passive displays, the controlling hardware, such as the lasers in upconversion displays, often cycles through cross-sections or even individual voxels, limiting the number of voxels that can be addressed while maintaining fluent motion. These shortcomings make it challenging for volumetric displays to match the size and resolution of conventional modern 2D displays. This due to the fundamental geometric fact that the number of voxels required in a volumetric display with a given voxel density scales cubically with its size, while the number of required pixels in a 2D display scales only quadratically, which is difficult to overcome.

Although volumetric displays only viewable from one side exist \cite{Sullivan_2002}, a capability unique to volumetric displays is the possibility for the view volume to be observed from all directions (except typically from below). Some volumetric displays even permit the user to enter the view volume \cite{saito2008laser}. Returning to the plenoptic function as a benchmark for 3D displays, this means that the domain of the approximation $\tilde{P}_v(x, y, z, \theta, \phi, \lambda, t)$ of the plenoptic function $P$ regarding some volumetric display $v$, is much larger in the directional dimensions $\theta$ and $\phi$ than the approximation $\tilde{P}_p$ of some planar (multiscopic or holographic) display $p$. While the $\theta, \phi$ domain of volumetric light field approximations is larger, the approximate values across that domain are less accurate, because volumetric displays are generally isotropic. Ideally, every voxel at a given $x, y, z$ location emits the same spectrum and brightness in all directions. Deviations from this are caused by technical reasons and cannot be controlled to display content. Conversely, holographic and multiview displays are directional emitters by design, which enables them to simulate view dependent effects such as reflections and specular highlights. The isotropic nature of volumetric displays also means that they are not capable of  Compared specifically to a multiview display $m$ with approximation $\tilde{P}_m$ within their respective $x, y, z$ domains, the accuracy of $\tilde{P}_m$ is proportional to the angular resolution (number of views) of $m$ \textit{and} its spatial resolution (pixel density) while the accuracy of $\tilde{P}_p$ depends \textit{only} on the spatial resolution (voxel density) of the volumetric display $v$. An equivalent, less formal explanation in terms of depth cues is that multiview displays provide discrete motion parallax through discrete views while volumetric displays inherently provide continuous motion parallax.

\subsection{Holographic displays}
Holography is a process by which a light field is recorded and reproduced. It is similar to photography in that it captures the light emitted by a scene on a two dimensional surface. They key distinction between holograms and two dimensional pictures recorded by a camera is that holograms store not only the amplitude or radiance of light incident across all points on the recording surface, but also its direction. This enables holography to get function without a camera pinhole and capture light from all directions simultaneously. The result is not a projection of a single 2D view or any finite number of views, but a representation of the four dimensional light field sampled at the recording surface \cite{Paschotta_2019}. Since no material is known that is sensitive to the direction of light \cite{benton2008holographic}, holography captures direction indirectly through interference between two phase coherent light sources: The reference beam illuminates the holographic plate directly while the object beam is reflected by the scene to be recorded. Since the beams have the same wavelength and phase, they form an interference pattern on the holographic plate.

Figure \ref{fig:zoneplate} is a contrived example of the simplest possible hologram recording. A curved wavefront from a point source representing the object interferes with a planar reference wave and forms a sinusoidal pattern on the holographic film. Because the object wave is curved and the reference wave is planar, the angle between them is zero at the center of the plate and increases towards the edges. In the interference pattern, this change in angle leads to sinusoidal fringes as the two wavefronts go into and out of phase. Crucially, the frequency of those fringes is proportional to the angle between the object wave and the reference wave. Real holograms can be thought of as combinations of infinitely many such sinusoidal fringe patterns; one for each point in the recorded scene.

What makes this useful for display technology is that this interference pattern can serve as a diffraction grating that reproduces the object's wavefront when exposed only to the reference wavefront \cite{Jesacher_Ritsch-Marte_2016}.
