\section{Fundamentals}
The properties of the ultimate display follow from human vision. Before discussing display technology, it is therefore necessary to understand exactly why a two-dimensional image of an object looks less convincing than the real thing. This question can be approached from two angles: One can analyze what aspects of human vision are not 'fooled' by a 2D display, or one can examine the physical differences between the light field produced by a 2D display and the light field that would be produced by the object displayed. The perception-centric approach is more focused on what matters subjectively to an observer, but runs the risk of missing something important, while the physically-based approach is more complete and objective, but may get caught up in details that are irrelevant to human perception.
\subsection{Depth perception}
Depth perception is typically analyzed in terms of various contributing factors called depth cues.

    \label{tab:depth-cues}
    \begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{l Y Y}
        \toprule
        & \textbf{Monocular} & \textbf{Binocular} \\ 
        \midrule
        \textbf{Visual} & Pictorial cues,\par Motion parallax & Retinal disparity \\  
        \midrule
        \textbf{Oculomotor} & Accommodation & Vergence \\
        \bottomrule
    \end{tabularx}
\end{table}
To better understand the requirements for emulating them in a 3D display, depth cues can be divided into four groups according to two criteria (Table \ref{tab:depth-cues}). Visual cues arise from the visual stimuli themselves, while oculomotor cues are sensed by proprioceptors in the eye muscles as they work to obtain the best possible view. Binocular cues are extracted from differences between the views seen by the left and the right eye, while monocular cues are inferred from a single view. Visual monocular cues are mainly pictorial cues such as occlusion, linear perspective, texture, shading, and prior knowledge. Pictorial cues are also commonly referred to as psychological cues, while all other cues are considered physiological. They are called pictorial cues because they can be conveyed via static 2D images as seen in conventional 2D displays, paintings or print. The exception is motion parallax, which describes the relative motion of objects across the field of view during lateral changes in perspective. The closer an object, the faster it crosses the field of view, since it widens toward the horizon. Retinal disparity, a visual binocular cue, arises from the slight difference in perspective between the left and the right eye. Light originating from the same point in space does not necessarily reach corresponding points on the left and right retinas. A detailed explanation of the geometry is given by Patterson and Martin (1992) \cite{patterson1992human}. Accommodation refers to subconscious adjustments made to the optical power of the eyes to obtain a clear view. How much the eye lenses must be stretched to obtain focus depends on the distance of the viewed object. This process functions as a depth cue even when only one eye is used or available, making accommodation a monocular cue. In contrast, vergence is the coordinated movement of the eyeballs to look at an object. For far away vergence points, the sight lines are almost parallel. The closer the vergence point gets, the more they must turn inward. This allows vergence to act as a binocular depth cue.

\subsection{The plenoptic function and light fields}

The plenoptic function mathematically describes light in terms of the radiance observed from a given position at coordinates $x, y, z$, in the direction through angles $\theta$ and $\phi$, at wavelength $\lambda$ and time $t$ \cite{bergen1991plenoptic}.

$$
P(x, y, z, \theta, \phi, \lambda, t)
$$


In the context of display technology, it is this function that the ultimate display should approximate. Since the radiance along a line does not change unless the light is blocked, the five parameters encoding observation position and direction may be reduced to four coordinates $u, v, s, t$ representing a line by its points of intersection with two arbitrarily placed planes \cite{levoy2023light}. If a time-varied light spectrum is defined for each line within the view volume enclosed by these planes, the resulting light field is equivalent to the values of the plenoptic function.
