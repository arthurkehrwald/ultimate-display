\section{Fundamentals}
The properties of the ultimate display follow from human vision. Before discussing display technology, it is therefore necessary to understand exactly why a two-dimensional image of an object looks less convincing than the real thing. This question can be approached from two angles: One can analyze what aspects of human vision are not 'fooled' by a 2D display, or one can examine the physical differences between the light field produced by a 2D display and the light field that would be produced by the object displayed. The perception-centric approach is more focused on what matters subjectively to an observer, but runs the risk of missing something important, while the physically-based approach is more complete and objective, but may get caught up in details that are irrelevant to human perception.
\subsection{Depth perception}
Depth perception is typically analyzed in terms of various contributing factors called depth cues.
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{l p{2.5cm} p{2.5cm}}
 & \textbf{Monocular} & \textbf{Binocular} \\ 
 \hline
 \textbf{Visual} & Pictorial cues & Retinal disparity \\  
 \hline
 \textbf{Oculomotor} & Accommodation & Vergence \\
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
To better understand the requirements for emulating them in a 3D display, depth cues can be divided into four groups according to two criteria. Visual cues arise from the visual stimuli themselves, while oculomotor cues are sensed by proprioceptors in the eye muscles as they work to optimize these stimuli. Binocular cues are extracted from differences between the views seen by the left and the right eye, while monocular cues are inferred from a single view. Visual monocular cues are pictorial cues such as occlusion, linear perspective, texture, shading, and prior knowledge. They are called pictorial cues because they can be conveyed by conventional 2D displays, paintings or in print. Retinal disparity, a visual binocular cue, arises from the slight difference in perspective between the left and the right eye. Light originating from the same point in space does not necessarily reach corresponding points on the left and right retinas. A detailed explanation of the geometry is given by Patterson and Martin (1992). \cite{patterson1992human} Accommodation refers to subconscious adjustments made to the optical power of the eyes to obtain a clear view. How much the eye lenses must be stretched to obtain focus depends on the distance of the viewed object. This process functions as a depth cue even when only one eye is used or available, making accommodation a monocular cue. In contrast, vergence is the coordinated movement of the eyeballs to look at an object. For far away vergence points, the eyeballs are almost parallel. The closer the vergence point gets, the more they must turn inward. This allows vergence to act as binocular depth cue.
